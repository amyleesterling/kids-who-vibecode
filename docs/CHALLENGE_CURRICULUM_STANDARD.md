# Challenge Curriculum Standard

## Audience and voice

Write for an intelligent 8–10-year-old: clear, warm, and a little surprising. Do not use baby talk, school-assignment theater, manipulative urgency, scarcity, or winning as the main reason to build. Failure may be funny; iteration is normal.

The child is creative lead. Code, blocks, templates, and AI are materials. A grown-up may support reading, accounts, typing, safety, and debugging, but should not become the uncredited builder.

## Required challenge record

Every primary contains identity/year/week/arc/version/state, complete copy, four schedule dates, age and time, creative skills and coding concepts, tools and visible free alternatives, parent/safety/accessibility notes, AI guidance, examples, submission requirements, risk flags, a visual brief, newsletter package, and adult editorial package.

Every primary also contains:

- Spark: one satisfying interaction in 15–30 minutes;
- Build: the 45–120 minute core;
- Glow-Up: a truly optional extension;
- a no/low-code route;
- one copyable grown-up-supervised AI prompt;
- one “make it yours” question;
- one pre-publish safety check;
- one reflection question about a creative decision.

## Annual balance

The 2027 sequence deliberately rotates formats: interactive art, narrative, simulation, puzzle, mapping, data art, exhibit, cooperative game, debugging tool, music-optional toy, generative nonsense, microgame, and reflective curation. Changing decorations does not count as a new mechanic.

At least one free route is equally visible. A paid product, public GitHub account, social account, real map, microphone, camera, face, voice, personal story, precise location, or public identity can never be required.

## Age-fit rubric

| Check | Green draft | Escalate or revise |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | Short instructions; new terms explained in context | Dense multi-clause procedure or unexplained jargon |
| Cognitive load | One core mechanic; small complete version first | Several systems must work before anything satisfying happens |
| Time | Spark works in one short sitting | Success assumes many adult-coded hours |
| Creative agency | Child chooses rules, characters, words, art, and tradeoffs | Template or AI makes the meaningful decisions |
| Access | Paper/offline/blocks/local-web route exists | Paid account or advanced deployment is essential |
| Failure | Reset, hints, and forgiving outcomes | Shame, elimination, streak loss, or public ranking pressure |

## Accessibility floor

- Never encode meaning by color or sound alone.
- Provide labels, readable contrast, simple controls, and a reset.
- Prefer keyboard and tap access; avoid precision dragging as the only path.
- Avoid flashing; supply pause or reduced motion for busy animation.
- Make sound optional and represent the same information visually.
- Do not require fast reactions for the core experience.

## AI-assistance floor

The prompt tells AI to ask one short question at a time, start from the child’s idea, explain changes, ask before adding code, and never invent the project’s voice. It prohibits names, faces, voices, schools, locations, contacts, accounts, tracking, payments, downloads, and external data. Adults preview the tool’s current privacy terms before use.

## Review evidence

Automated validators check structural completeness, unique mechanics/titles/prompts, schedule order, package completeness, and hard safety wording. Curriculum, age-fit, accessibility, and inclusion records remain distinct. A passing validator is evidence of structure—not proof of delight, cultural suitability, legal compliance, or production readiness.
